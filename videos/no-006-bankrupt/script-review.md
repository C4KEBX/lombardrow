# Script review: No. 6 Why 'bankrupt' means 'broken bench'

**Score: 64/100**: The facts are strong and the ending loops well, but the hanging hook gives way to a 16-second word lesson, the trunk scene buries its one detail that proves he hid the money, and Perrott arrives with no reason to care about him. Not ready to build yet.

Word count now: 195 written words (the draft estimates 70 s). After all three fixes: 195 written words (fix 1: +2, fix 2: +3, fix 3: -5). Fix 3 also cuts "November 11th", about 1 s of spoken numbers, so the read gets slightly shorter and stays inside 62 to 70 s.

## The three fixes that would raise the score most

1. **Make the hook promise a person, then pay it off at Perrott. About +5 points (Hook +3, Story +2).**
   - `hook`, as written: "A bankrupt who hid his money could hang, in 18th-century England. Even the word hides a break." (17 words)
   - Rewrite: "A bankrupt who hid his money could hang. In 18th-century England, one London merchant did. Even the word hides a break." (21 words)
   - `perrott`, as written: "Meet John Perrott, a merchant on Ludgate Hill, in London." (10 words)
   - Rewrite: "That merchant was John Perrott, of Ludgate Hill." (8 words). Change the microhook to "That merchant was John Perrott," and move the `London` shot's `atWord` to `Hill`.
   - Why: the claim lands within about 2.5 s with nothing in front of it. "One London merchant did" opens a "who?" that carries the viewer through the etymology section and is closed at about 30 s. "Meet John Perrott" is a scroll-away line that introduces a stranger. As rewritten, the scene pays off the hook.
   - Facts: f-perrott-shop (John Perrott, of Ludgate Hill, London, Merchant), f-perrott-hanged (hanged for fraudulent bankruptcy), f-1706-capital (fraudulent bankruptcy means hiding assets from creditors). The hanging is in the 18th century (1761). The on-screen hookLine stays as it is.

2. **Keep the trunk detail that shows concealment, and stop the pictures arguing with it. About +4 points (Payoff +2, Clarity +1, Picture +1).**
   - `trunk`, as written: "In his trunk in Newgate prison, halves of four banknotes turned up, tied in a rag. Plus half a 1,000 pound note." (22 words)
   - Rewrite: "In Newgate prison, at the bottom of his trunk, tied in a rag: the other halves of four banknotes. And half a 1,000 pound note." (25 words)
   - Why: "at the bottom" and "the other halves" are both in the source and both were cut. They turn "some banknotes turned up" into "he had them stashed", which is the line a viewer repeats to a friend. Ending on "the other halves" also gives the scene a beat to land on.
   - Picture: the `1,000` shot lands on `boe-note`, which assets.json lists as a **£70** note. If that crop shows the denomination, the picture contradicts the voice. Crop away from any printed value, or hold on the rag/halves crop. The `newgate-1780` picture shows the 1780 Gordon riots with fire and a crowd. Under "at the bottom of his trunk" that reads as a riot. Crop tight to the masonry, away from the flames.
   - Facts: f-half-notes, word for word ("the other halves of four banknotes were found tied up in a rag at the bottom of Perrott's trunk in Newgate prison, along with half of a 1,000-pound note"). The script never explains why he was in Newgate or why the notes were cut in half. Both need a source, so don't add either.

3. **Cut the filler in the word section and the hard-to-hear date, which pays for fixes 1 and 2. About +3 points (Story +2, Clarity +1).**
   - `myth`, as written: "Legend says a failed moneylender's bench was smashed. But here's the catch: the dictionary says that's probably a figure of speech." (21 words)
   - Rewrite: "Legend says a failed moneylender's bench was smashed. The dictionary says that's probably just a figure of speech." (18 words). Retarget the `catch` shot to `dictionary` and drop the "But here's the catch:" microhook. "Here's the catch" is generic filler and slows the scene exactly when the viewer is waiting to get back to the hanging.
   - `hanged`, as written: "On November 11th, 1761, Perrott was hanged at Smithfield for fraudulent bankruptcy." (12 words)
   - Rewrite: "In 1761, Perrott was hanged at Smithfield for fraudulent bankruptcy." (10 words). Before this cut, the 20 s from `short` to `four` has five numbers (1760, 13,513, 1,000, November 11th 1761, 1820). The day of the month adds nothing, and cutting it means the climax lands on "hanged" without a date to get through first.
   - Facts: f-bench-myth ("said to come from an old custom of breaking the bench... probably is figurative", with the "probably" hedge kept) and f-perrott-hanged.

## What works
- The hook line is true, deadly, and visual, and it is on screen and in the cover from frame one.
- The "Hide it. / Hang for it." kinetic lines (`law-1706`) are the best on-screen text in the script. The "hid / hides / hiding" motif ties the word, the law and the crime together.
- "And that law hid one more idea." is planted at about 29 s and paid off in `fresh-start` with "Almost an afterthought", a real and surprising sourced twist.
- The hedges are kept in the narration: "what's considered its first", "probably a figure of speech", "Likely only 4".
- The ending question is a real moral choice and is not financial advice. The last frame matches the first.

## Accuracy flags
- `law-1542` on-screen lines "First / bankruptcy law" drop the hedge the narration keeps ("what's considered"; the fact says "what scholars generally consider"). With the sound off, it reads as settled fact. Make it "England's first?" or add "considered".
- `fresh-start`: "The discharge lasted." is a little stronger than f-discharge ("would have a fruitful future"). It is acceptable, but don't push it further, for example to "still exists today" (needs a source).
- `fresh-start`: "a fresh start" as the meaning of discharge comes from plan.json's angle, not from facts.json. As wording in the closing question it is low risk. As a claim ("discharge means a fresh start") it needs a source.
- `trunk`: why Perrott was in Newgate, and why the notes were halved, needs a source. Don't explain either without one.
- Picture: if the `boe-note` crop at "1,000" shows "70", it mislabels the words (see fix 2).
- Otherwise none. Every narrated claim traces to a fact id in `speaks`.
